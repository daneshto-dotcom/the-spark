SPARK · S192 · independent research audit · 1 October 2026

Engine or no engine?

How SPARK is built today, what it would cost to move it into Godot, Unity or Unreal, and the cheaper path that fixes the effects without throwing anything away. Every repo number on this page was measured from the main checkout today. Every price and version was checked online today, with sources at the bottom.

The short answer
  1. Don't rebuild. The ugly effects aren't the web stack's fault. SPARK draws its effects as flat, solid shapes, with no glow, no additive light and no particle textures. Pixi, the drawing library we already use, can do all of that.
  2. This month: run a visual pilot on the current stack. Redo Vlad's siphon and the building aura with soft particle textures, additive blending and a bloom filter. That's one or two days of work and costs $0 (MIT-licensed libraries, free CC0 textures).
  3. For Steam: wrap the same game in Electron and add Steamworks. Vampire Survivors (Phaser + Electron) sold about 2 million copies in its first month this way, and Cookie Clicker ships on Steam as an Electron app. Expect 1–2 weeks of work, plus Steam's fixed 2-week "Coming Soon" wait.
  4. An engine port would take roughly 3–6 months before SPARK is back where it is today. You'd rewrite about 110,000 lines of game code and 115,000 lines of tests, and you'd re-open bug classes the project has spent five months closing.
  5. Reconsider an engine only if SPARK becomes a different game, for example 3D, a console target, or a team of artists working in an editor. Godot would be the engine, not Unity or Unreal.

1 · Today's stack, measured from the repo

You're right that we don't use a game engine. SPARK is a web app written in TypeScript, and it draws the screen with PixiJS, a 2D drawing library that browser games use. It does sprites, shapes, filters and shaders, but it has no editor, no particle designer and no asset store. Everything an engine's editor would give you, we write as code.

How effects are drawn today

The render folder splits into two kinds of drawing:

Code-drawn shapes (Pixi "Graphics")
  • 81 of 121 render modules draw with circles, lines and polygons
  • 59 of them use nothing but shapes: no images at all
  • 388 direct shape-drawing calls
  • Includes every boss ability, building aura, hit flash, bomb, bond, toast and health bar
Picture-based art (sprites, atlases)
  • 28 modules load images or atlases
  • 69 animation atlases across public/art (19 folders) and public/godly (12)
  • Characters, towers, castles, zones; three VFX atlases (Ra strike, pentagram, lightning hub)
  • The veo pipeline: one still → image-to-video clip → 12 frames per row (not 24) packed by scripts/build-sprite-atlas.mjs
The real reason the effects look cheap: I searched the whole src/ tree. There are zero glow or bloom filters, zero additive blend modes (the setting that makes overlapping particles add up to light, the way fire and magic do), zero particle systems, and zero soft particle textures. The only custom shader in the game is the luma-key that cuts the background out of cutscene video. Every effect is a set of solid, flat-coloured circles whose transparency is animated. Engines don't make effects look good by magic. They make the right defaults (additive, soft-edged, glowing) easy to reach for. Pixi has the same defaults; nobody has used them yet.

The architecture, and why it's the expensive part to port

SPARK is host-authoritative. One player's browser runs the real match (the "sim") and sends everyone else snapshots 10 times a second. The sim is also deterministic: given the same inputs it produces exactly the same match, tick for tick. There's no randomness, no clock and no drifting decimals, and every target scan has a strict tie-break order. That's what lets the project replay matches, run the sim in a background worker, and compare two sims by hash.

That machinery lives in src/state (130 files, 41,221 lines) plus the game, physics and bot code, about 50,700 lines in total. Behind it sit 115,000 lines of tests and a long list of hard-won rules in CLAUDE.md: the four-sites rule, the stat ladder, protocol versions. None of that is graphics, and none of it would get easier in an engine. An engine gives you a better way to draw. It doesn't give you SPARK's rules. Porting means re-typing all 50,700 lines into another language and re-proving them, and the tests that do the proving would have to be rewritten too.

What it would take to fix the two named effects

Vlad's siphon, redone
  1. A soft round particle texture (Kenney, or one generated image), drawn with additive blending, so overlapping motes build into bright red light instead of flat dots.
  2. About 120 particles spiralling inward along curved paths, not 18 sliding straight. Each leaves a short trail (MeshRope).
  3. A bloom/glow filter on the effects layer, so the core and the streams bleed light.
  4. A dark-red ground stain that pulses under him, and a shockwave ripple (Shockwave filter) the tick the heal lands.
  5. Still driven purely by world.tick and the synced sapFlashUntilTick, so every player sees the same thing, as now.

Effort: 1–2 days. Bundle: under 15 KiB. No new art spend.

Building aura, redone
  1. Swap the 2 px rings for a soft textured ring (one radial-gradient image) that expands and fades, drawn additively.
  2. A slow rune or sigil decal per race rotating under the tower. One generated still per race, rotated in code, no video needed.
  3. A few rising embers or motes in the owner's colour from the same deterministic emitter.
  4. Keep the existing towerCover fade rules exactly as they are (S183).

Effort: 1–2 days after the siphon, since it reuses the emitter and the glow layer. Art: 6 stills (one per race), not 6 videos.

3 · Option B: rebuild in an engine

An engine gives you three things we don't have: an editor where you see and tweak an effect live, an asset store with drop-in packs, and built-in Steam/console export. Here's what each would actually mean for this game.

Godot 4.x (you know it; latest stable 4.7.2, Aug 2026)

  • VFX: a real 2D particle editor (GPUParticles2D), a visual shader editor, and AnimationPlayer for timing. This is the strongest argument for it: you could tune an effect yourself without a coding session.
  • Asset library: free and smaller than Unity's. For a 2D top-down TD/builder you get tools (particle packs, the Kenney packs, shader collections) more than finished game art. Paid 2D art comes from itch.io / CraftPix anyway, and those work in Pixi too.
  • 2D: excellent; a true 2D engine.
  • Multiplayer: high-level multiplayer API with host authority built in. In the browser it supports WebRTC and WebSocket, but you'd still need your own signalling server; no raw sockets. Since 4.3, single-threaded web export is the default and the most compatible. C# projects can't be exported to the web, so GDScript only. Browser play survives, at roughly 35 MB+ of engine download (your Pitch Masters build).
  • Steam: GodotSteam (MIT; version 4.20 shipped alongside Godot 4.7) covers achievements, lobbies and Steam networking through Valve's relays, which would replace the TURN/relay troubles. Desktop builds only, not web.
  • Licence: MIT. Free forever, no royalty, no seat fee.
  • Proof: Brotato (Godot) passed 10 million copies; Slay the Spire 2 moved from Unity to Godot and entered Early Access in March 2026.

Unity 6

  • VFX: Shuriken particles and VFX Graph; the biggest market of ready-made effect packs.
  • Asset store: the largest, with many 2D VFX and TD kits, mostly built for Unity's own particle system.
  • 2D: good (2D renderer, 2D lights, Sprite Shape).
  • Multiplayer: Netcode for GameObjects runs on Unity Transport. In a browser (Unity Web/WebGL) it can only be a WebSocket client, so a player's browser can't be the host the way SPARK's is. Browser multiplayer would need a paid relay or dedicated server. That breaks SPARK's free P2P model on the web.
  • Steam: mature (Steamworks.NET, Facepunch.Steamworks) on desktop.
  • Licence and cost: Personal is free under $200K/yr revenue + funding, and from Unity 6 the "Made with Unity" splash is optional. Pro is $2,310/yr per seat (from 12 Jan 2026). The per-install "Runtime Fee" was announced in Sept 2023 and cancelled on 12 Sep 2024. It's gone, but it's why studios such as Mega Crit left.

Unreal Engine 5

  • VFX: Niagara is the best particle system on this list, but it's built for 3D.
  • 2D: weak, and that's true. Paper2D still ships, but it has had little attention for years. 2D work relies on the paid Paper ZD plugin or on faking 2D in 3D.
  • Multiplayer: excellent dedicated-server replication. No browser export at all; HTML5 support was removed back in 4.24. spark-online.space would end.
  • Licence: free until a product's lifetime gross reaches $1 million, then 5% royalty on gross (waived on Epic Games Store sales).
  • Verdict: the wrong tool for a 2D top-down builder. Not recommended.

4 · Option C: hybrid. Same web game, shipped to Steam

Package the existing build as a desktop app. Steam players download it like any other game; it runs the same Pixi code inside a bundled browser (Chromium).

Caveat: the in-game Steam overlay is unreliable in Electron. The known workaround is to keep a canvas redrawing every frame, which a game already does. Maintenance is slow, so budget time to patch it yourself. Alternative: greenworks.

Has this actually worked?

GameTech on SteamResult
Vampire SurvivorsPhaser (JavaScript) in Electron, until it moved to Unity in v1.6 (Aug 2023) for console ports and performanceAbout 2 million copies and about $7M in its first month on Steam, all on web tech
Cookie ClickerThe HTML game in an Electron wrapper (Steam, Sep 2021), with achievements, cloud saves and WorkshopEstimated 2.5M+ copies on Steam (estimates vary widely)
CrossCodeModified impact.js + NW.jsEstimated about 464K copies and about $6M gross on Steam; later ported to consoles

What Steam needs from us: a $100 Steam Direct fee (returned after $1,000 in revenue), a store page reviewed in about 3–5 business days, that page live as "Coming Soon" for at least 2 weeks, then a build review. Achievements, cloud saves and lobbies come through steamworks.js. Multiplayer could use Steam lobbies + Steam's relay network on the Steam build. That ends the TURN and relay problems for Steam players, while the web build keeps Trystero. The transport behind our snapshot protocol would need a second implementation (estimate: 1–2 weeks).

Performance: the same as Chrome today. The one thing to fix before Steam is the 4.8–6.8 MB boss PNG atlases. Compressing them (WebP/KTX2) cuts load time and GPU memory. That's a packaging job, not a rewrite.

The ceiling: consoles. Switch, PlayStation and Xbox don't run Electron. If consoles ever become a goal, that's the moment an engine port starts paying for itself, which is exactly what Vampire Survivors did after it was already a hit.

The call: A now, C next, B only if the game's goals change.
  • Biggest visual jump per hour this month: Option A. The effects are the weakest part, the cheapest to fix, and fully under Pixi's control. The gain comes from switching on additive light, soft textures and bloom, none of which SPARK uses today.
  • Stop generating video for effects. Keep veo for characters and towers, which are things with a body. Effects should be particles + glow + shaders that react to the game. That also cuts the $20-a-clip spend.
  • Steam is reachable without an engine. Electron + steamworks.js is a proven path with multi-million-copy precedents.
  • If you ever do rebuild, use Godot: MIT, real 2D, you know it, browser export still works, and GodotSteam is solid. Unity's browser can't host; Unreal has no browser and weak 2D.
